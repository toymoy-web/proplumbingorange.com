Buying an Old Towne Home? Check the Plumbing First

A charming Craftsman or Victorian in Old Towne often comes with plumbing that's older than most buyers realize. Here's what actually matters before you sign.

Written by the Orange Pro Plumbing team. Published September 2026.

1880s-1940s construction.Clay laterals.Galvanized supply lines.

Why Old Towne's plumbing age matters more than the house's age alone.

Old Towne Orange has some of the oldest housing stock in the county, with homes dating from the 1880s through the 1940s. A well-maintained century-old home can be a genuinely great buy, but the plumbing underneath it is a different question from the house's cosmetic condition.

Two materials come up again and again in these homes: clay sewer laterals, often original to the house, and galvanized steel supply lines, common until the mid-20th century. Neither fails on a predictable schedule, but both are well past the point where failure becomes a real, not theoretical, possibility.

What to actually check before closing.

A camera inspection of the sewer lateral is the single most useful pre-purchase plumbing check for an Old Towne home. It shows whether the line has root intrusion, offset joints, or a partial collapse, none of which are visible from the house itself. Sewer line repair after the fact costs meaningfully more than a heads-up before you own the problem.

For supply lines, water pressure and discoloration are the practical checks a buyer can do themselves before bringing in a professional. Run several fixtures at once and see if pressure drops noticeably, and check for rust-colored water after the house has sat unused for a few hours. Neither test is definitive, but both are useful early signals.

The two most common surprises.

Recurring drain clogs that seemed like a minor annoyance to the seller are often root intrusion into a clay lateral, which is a repair, not a maintenance item. If a seller mentions needing to snake a drain "every year or so," that's worth investigating rather than dismissing.

The second common surprise is a home that's had one section of supply line replaced, often visible as a patch of newer copper or PEX spliced into older galvanized pipe. That's not necessarily a red flag, it usually means a past leak was fixed, but it's worth asking when and why.

When a full repipe makes more sense than repairs.

If an inspection turns up multiple unrelated issues, a slow leak here, corrosion there, it's often more cost-effective to repipe the home's supply lines than to keep fixing individual failures as they come up. This is a common enough situation in Old Towne that it's worth budgeting for as a possibility, not treating it as a worst-case scenario.

None of this should scare a buyer away from Old Towne. Plenty of century-old homes here have been properly maintained or already repiped. The point is knowing which version of the house you're actually buying before the offer is final, the same way you'd check the roof or foundation.

What a plumber's pre-purchase visit actually covers.

A dedicated plumbing inspection before closing typically includes a full camera run of the sewer lateral, a pressure test on supply lines, and a visual check of any accessible pipe in the garage, crawlspace, or under sinks. It takes an hour or two in most cases.

The output isn't a pass or fail, it's a real picture of what you're taking on. Some buyers use that information to negotiate a credit for known issues, others simply want to go in with accurate expectations rather than surprises in the first year of ownership.

What happens if you skip this step.

Buyers who skip a plumbing-specific inspection sometimes find out about a sewer or supply line issue the hard way, during the first significant rain, or the first time a fixture is run at full capacity for an extended period. Any eventual repair still needs to meet California Plumbing Code standards, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code, regardless of when it happens.

By that point, the repair is a post-purchase expense rather than a pre-purchase negotiation point, and it often costs more to fix urgently than it would have to address calmly with advance notice.

A realistic budget line to plan around.

Even with a clean inspection, it's reasonable to set aside a contingency for plumbing work in the first year or two of owning a pre-1950s Old Towne home, simply because original materials this old don't always announce a problem before it happens.

This isn't about assuming the worst. It's the same logic as budgeting for a roof or an aging HVAC system in an older home: a known category of expense that's easier to plan for than to be surprised by.

Common questions.

What if the home has already been repiped by a previous owner?

Ask for documentation and, if possible, confirm the work was permitted. A repipe done without permits carries the same uncertainty as any other unpermitted plumbing work.

Is a 100-year-old clay lateral automatically a problem?

Not automatically, some are still intact and functioning. Age raises the odds of an issue but doesn't guarantee one, which is exactly why inspection matters more than assumption.

Do all Old Towne homes need the same level of scrutiny?

Homes that have already been updated or repiped need less scrutiny than ones with clearly original systems, so the inspection findings matter more than the home's age alone.

Can I ask the seller to fix plumbing issues found during inspection?

Yes, this is a normal part of negotiation. Whether the seller agrees depends on the specific market and the nature of what's found.

How much does a pre-purchase plumbing inspection typically cost?

It varies, but it's a small fraction of the cost of an unexpected repair after closing, which is why most buyers consider it worthwhile.

What if the seller won't allow a plumbing inspection before closing?

That's worth treating as a real signal. A seller confident in the home's condition typically has no objection to a buyer verifying it.

Should I get a plumbing inspection separate from a general home inspection?

For a pre-1950s Old Towne home, yes. A general home inspector typically checks visible plumbing but doesn't run a sewer camera or pressure-test supply lines the way a plumber would.
