What License Should Your Orange County Plumber Actually Have?

Not every person who shows up with a truck and tools is legally allowed to do plumbing work in California. Here's what the license actually verifies.

Written by the Orange Pro Plumbing team. Published September 2026.

C-36 license.CSLB requirements.What to ask for.

The basic rule.

California requires a C-36 plumbing contractor license for any plumbing work totaling $500 or more in combined labor and materials. Below that threshold, minor work is technically exempt, but most real plumbing jobs, a repair, an installation, anything beyond the smallest fix, exceed it.

What actually goes into getting the license.

A C-36 license isn't handed out for owning tools. It requires documented experience working under a licensed plumber, typically several years, along with passing both a trade exam covering plumbing knowledge and a separate law and business exam covering California contractor requirements.

Licensed contractors also carry a contractor's bond, which exists specifically to give homeowners some financial recourse if a licensed contractor's work fails to meet standards or the contractor doesn't fulfill their obligations.

Why this matters beyond just "following the rules".

The exam and experience requirements exist because plumbing work done incorrectly, particularly gas line work, has real safety consequences, not just a risk of a leak coming back. A license is a baseline signal that someone has demonstrated the knowledge, not a guarantee of quality on its own, but a real floor worth checking for.

Work performed to California Plumbing Code standards, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code, also matters for anything that requires a permit and inspection, like plumbing installation for a remodel. Unlicensed work can create real problems at resale time if it was never permitted or inspected correctly.

What to do before you hire.

Ask for the license number and verify it directly through the California Contractors State License Board's public lookup rather than just taking a business's word for it. A legitimate contractor won't hesitate to provide this.

It's also reasonable to ask whether a specific job, like sewer line repair involving excavation, requires a permit, and to expect the contractor to handle that permit as part of the job rather than leaving it to you.

What happens if you hire unlicensed help anyway.

Beyond the safety risk, unlicensed plumbing work can create real problems later: it typically isn't permitted or inspected, which can complicate a home sale when a buyer's inspector or the title process turns up unpermitted work.

If something goes wrong with unlicensed work, there's no contractor's bond or licensing board to appeal to, which leaves a homeowner with far less recourse than they'd have with a licensed professional.

What this looks like in practice for a homeowner.

When you call for a quote, a legitimate licensed plumber will typically provide their license number without being asked twice, explain what permits (if any) a job requires, and give you a written, itemized estimate rather than a vague verbal number.

None of this takes extra time on a normal call. If a business is reluctant to provide basic licensing information, that reluctance itself is worth treating as a signal.

How this applies to larger jobs specifically.

For bigger projects like whole-home repiping or sewer line replacement, licensing matters even more, since these jobs involve permits, inspections, and code compliance that an unlicensed operator typically can't or won't handle correctly.

It's reasonable to ask a contractor directly how they plan to handle permitting on a larger job before work begins, not as an accusation, but as a normal part of hiring for a project of that scope.

Common questions.

Is the license number required to appear on invoices?

Yes, licensed contractors are required to include their license number on contracts and invoices in California.

Does a C-36 license cover septic system work too?

No, septic work typically falls under a separate specialty classification, worth confirming if that's part of your project.

Can I check a contractor's complaint history along with their license?

Yes, the CSLB's public lookup also shows any disciplinary history or complaints on file.

Does a C-36 license holder need separate certification for gas work?

Gas line work generally falls within the C-36 scope, though some jurisdictions have additional local permit requirements worth confirming.

Is a business license the same thing as a contractor license?

No, a general business license and a C-36 contractor license are separate requirements, and a plumbing business needs both to operate legally.

Does a C-36 license need to be renewed periodically?

Yes, California contractor licenses require periodic renewal along with continuing compliance with bonding and insurance requirements.

Can a licensed plumber lose their license for poor work?

Yes, the Contractors State License Board can take disciplinary action, including suspension or revocation, for documented violations.

Is it common for unlicensed work to pass a home inspection?

A general inspection may miss it, but unpermitted work often surfaces during a title search or a more thorough investigation, which is a real resale risk.

Does a C-36 license cover both residential and commercial plumbing?

Yes, the license itself covers plumbing contracting broadly, though individual businesses may specialize in one or the other.

Can a licensed plumber subcontract work to an unlicensed helper?

Work should be performed or directly supervised by licensed personnel. It's reasonable to ask who will actually be doing the work on your job.

Does license status affect insurance claims related to plumbing work?

It can. Some homeowner policies scrutinize claims involving unpermitted or unlicensed work more closely, which is another reason licensing matters beyond the work itself.

Does a licensed plumber cost more than an unlicensed handyman?

Sometimes, though the gap is often smaller than people assume, and it comes with real protections, including the contractor's bond and code-compliant work that won't cause resale issues later.

How do I verify a license number myself?

The California Contractors State License Board maintains a free public lookup tool where you can confirm a license is active and see its history.
