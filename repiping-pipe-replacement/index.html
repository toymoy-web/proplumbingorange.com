A second or third unrelated leak within a year or two is usually the clearest sign that a home's pipe material, not a single bad section, has reached the end of its service life. This shows up most in Old Towne's original galvanized and clay-era plumbing and in Eichler tract homes as their slab-embedded copper ages past 60 years. We repipe in copper or PEX depending on the home's layout, with the work staged to limit wall and ceiling damage. If you're not yet sure how widespread the problem is, a leak detection visit can confirm the scope before you commit to a full repipe.

Signs

How Orange homeowners describe it

Recurring pinhole leaks.

Multiple unrelated leaks over a short period usually means the pipe material itself is failing, not one bad section.

Discolored or metallic-tasting water.

A common sign of galvanized pipe corroding from the inside.

Steadily dropping water pressure.

Corrosion narrowing the inside of old pipes reduces flow throughout the house.

A home built before 1960 with original plumbing.

Old Towne homes this age are the most common repiping candidates in the city.

Process

How a repipe actually happens

System assessment.

We check pipe material, wall access, and fixture count to scope the job accurately.

Material choice.

Copper or PEX, chosen based on your home's layout and budget, explained honestly rather than upsold.

Staged access.

Work is done in stages to keep water on where possible and minimize wall and ceiling damage.

Pressure test and patch.

Full system pressure test before any wall is closed, then patching handled or coordinated for you.

The Orange picture

Why Orange homes call about this

Old Towne's mixed-era plumbing.

Homes in Old Towne Orange can have patchwork repairs layering 1880s original galvanized supply lines with mid-century and modern fixes, which is exactly the pattern that makes a full repipe more cost-effective than another one-off repair.

Eichler copper reaching end of service life.

Original copper embedded in the slab in the Fairhaven and Fairhills tracts is now past 60 years old in many homes. Once it starts leaking in multiple spots, re-routing new lines above the slab is usually the better long-term fix than repeated slab access repairs.

Hard water shortens galvanized lifespan further.

At 16 to 18 grains of hardness, galvanized pipe scales and corrodes internally faster here than in soft-water regions, which is part of why homes in this city hit repiping age sooner than the pipe's rated lifespan would suggest.

Licensed work, real code compliance.

Whole-home repiping is exactly the kind of job California's C-36 licensing requirement exists for. It always exceeds the $500 threshold that requires a licensed contractor, and it has to meet California Plumbing Code standards, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code, for pipe sizing and material, not just pass a casual inspection.

Not sure what's wrong.

"I keep having leaks in different spots," when it's time to repipe

One leak is a repair. A second or third unrelated leak within a year or two, especially in a home with galvanized or original copper lines, usually means the pipe material itself has reached the end of its service life. At that point, repiping the affected section or the whole house costs less over time than repeated emergency repairs.

Material choice.

Copper.

  • Longer proven track record.
  • Higher upfront material cost.
  • Rigid, more joints in tight spaces.

PEX.

  • Faster install, fewer joints.
  • Lower material cost.
  • Flexible routing around slab obstacles.
